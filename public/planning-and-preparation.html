Checklist: Essential Permit Considerations for Your Remodel

  • Define project scope clearly — precisely identify what you plan to change, add, or modify in your home. This helps determine which permits are needed.
  • Review local building department guidelines — visit your specific city's building department website (e.g., Fort Worth) to understand their unique requirements for residential renovations.
  • Confirm who obtains permits — ensure your contract explicitly states that the general contractor is responsible for securing all necessary permits before construction begins.
  • Verify contractor's license and insurance — check that your chosen contractor is properly licensed and insured; this is often a prerequisite for obtaining permits and protects you.
  • Understand inspection requirements — know which stages of your project will require official inspections and how these will be scheduled and documented.
  • Anticipate review timelines — be aware that permit review periods can vary significantly; planning for potential delays is part of a realistic project schedule.
  • Document all changes to plans — any modifications to the initial approved plans must be properly documented and potentially re-permitted to avoid compliance issues.

How to Prepare Your Home for an Interior Refresh

Before any work begins, remove as much furniture, decor, and wall hangings as possible from the room. For larger items, move them to the center of the room and cover them thoroughly with plastic sheeting or drop cloths. Ensure floors are completely covered with sturdy drop cloths or paper to protect against spills and drips. This step is crucial for keeping your belongings safe and giving the team ample space to work efficiently.

Thoroughly clean all surfaces to be painted. Use a mild detergent and water to remove dirt, grease, and grime. Pay special attention to high-traffic areas like entryways and kitchens. After cleaning, inspect the walls and ceilings for any cracks, holes, or imperfections. Mark these areas so they can be addressed in the next step. A clean surface is essential for proper paint adhesion.

Fill any holes or cracks with spackle or joint compound, then sand them smooth once dry. For glossy surfaces, a light sanding will help the new paint adhere better. Don’t forget to caulk any gaps around trim or molding. For significant damage, or if you notice stains or moisture issues, this is when professional insight becomes important; painting alone won't solve underlying problems.

Use painter’s tape to create crisp lines around trim, windows, and ceilings. Apply primer to any patched areas, bare drywall, or surfaces with stains. Primer ensures a uniform base, prevents bleed-through, and promotes better paint adhesion and a more consistent final color. Don't skip this if you want a professional-looking finish.

Throughout the process, especially during painting and drying, maintain good ventilation by opening windows or using fans. Avoid painting in extremely cold or humid conditions, as this can negatively impact how the paint cures and its overall durability. Following the paint product's stated recoat times is also vital for the longevity of your finish. This careful preparation is key to achieving the high-end craftsmanship we value, ensuring your newly refreshed interiors stand the test of time.

  • Inadequate Surface Preparation: Don't skip cleaning, sanding, or repairing walls before painting. — How to avoid it: Thoroughly clean all surfaces to remove dirt and grease. Patch and sand any imperfections. Glossy surfaces should be scuffed to ensure proper paint adhesion. This is foundational for a durable finish.
  • Ignoring Product Drying Instructions: Many homeowners rush to apply a second coat before the first is fully cured. — How to avoid it: Always follow the paint manufacturer’s recommended recoat times. While paint may feel dry to the touch, it needs time to properly cure and achieve maximum durability. Rushing can lead to cracking or peeling.
  • Using Unsuitable Tools or Materials: Opting for cheap brushes, rollers, or paint can compromise the finish. — How to avoid it: Invest in quality tools and paints appropriate for your specific surfaces and desired sheen. Better tools provide smoother application and a more professional look. Don't compromise on the quality of your materials; it impacts the final craftsmanship.
  • Neglecting Primer for Repairs or Stains: Painting over unprimed patches or stains often leads to inconsistent color or bleed-through. — How to avoid it: Always spot-prime any repaired areas, bare drywall, or stubborn stains before applying your finish coats. Primer creates a uniform base and prevents imperfections from showing through.
  • Underestimating Environmental Factors: Painting in extreme temperatures or humidity can affect adhesion and curing. — How to avoid it: Ensure the room is well-ventilated and maintain moderate temperatures during painting and drying. Check the paint can for optimal temperature and humidity ranges. This is particularly important in the varied climate of the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex.

Before you drill anything, determine exactly where each knob or pull will go. Will all knobs be centered on the stile? Will pulls on upper cabinets be placed differently than those on lower cabinets or drawers? Consistency is crucial for a cohesive look. Map out your plan, perhaps even drawing temporary marks with a pencil.

Don't assume all hardware is identical or that standard measurements will work for every cabinet. Measure the center-to-center spacing of your pulls, not just their overall length. Also, measure the thickness of your cabinet doors and drawer fronts. This informs the correct screw length needed and helps confirm if existing holes can be reused or will need filling.

This step is a game-changer for consistency. You can buy an adjustable cabinet hardware jig or create your own template from a piece of scrap wood, cardboard, or plastic. Mark your chosen hardware placement on the template. This ensures every hole is drilled in the exact same spot, reducing errors across multiple cabinets.

Before drilling into your actual cabinets, always test your template on a scrap piece of wood or, for an inconspicuous area, the back of a less visible cabinet door. Once confident, use your template to mark the precise drilling points on each door and drawer front. A light dimple with an awl can help prevent the drill bit from wandering.

Select a drill bit slightly larger than your hardware screws. For wood, a twist bit works well; for other materials, you might need a specialized bit. Ensure you drill straight, not at an angle. If drilling through thin material, place a scrap wood block behind the drilling area to prevent splintering. Check for any hinge clearance issues before drilling.

Carefully thread the screws through the back of the cabinet door or drawer front and attach the knob or pull. Don't overtighten, as this can strip screws or damage the cabinet finish. Snug is good. Check that the hardware is level and firmly in place, then make any minor adjustments.

After installation, stand back and visually inspect all your newly installed hardware. Do the knobs and pulls look consistent? Do they align properly? Open and close each door and drawer to ensure the hardware doesn't snag or obstruct anything. For drawers, pulls are typically centered horizontally and vertically. While these are common conventions, your final layout should consider door style, cabinet dimensions, appliance clearances, and your preferred aesthetic for a customized renovation.

This is a critical step to avoid mistakes. Don't commit to drilling until you've tested the placement. Use painter's tape or a removable adhesive to temporarily affix one pull to an upper cabinet door and one to a lower drawer. Step back and observe. Does it feel comfortable to grasp? Does it look balanced? This visual confirmation allows you to adjust the placement before any permanent marks are made, ensuring you're happy with the outcome and minimizing the risk of misaligned hardware.

Once you've finalized your test placement, it's time to mark the drilling points. A reusable jig or template is invaluable here because it helps maintain consistent offsets across all your cabinets. Align the jig precisely according to your chosen standard, making sure the 'center-to-center' measurement matches your hardware. Mark the pilot hole locations with a pencil. Double-check these marks against your hardware to ensure they align perfectly. This precision is essential for a reliable installation.

Step 1: Understand Door and Drawer Construction — Before marking anything, take a good look at your cabinet doors and drawer fronts. Are they flat panel, raised panel, or Shaker-style? For Shaker or framed doors, knobs and pulls are typically placed on the stile (the vertical frame piece) rather than the recessed panel. On flat panels, you have more flexibility, but consistency is key.

Step 2: Choose a Consistent Reference Point — For upper cabinet doors, hardware is generally placed on the stile opposite the hinge, typically 2-4 inches up from the bottom corner. For lower cabinet doors, it’s usually 2-4 inches down from the top corner, again, on the stile opposite the hinge. For drawers, the center is a common choice, but ensure it aligns visually with the overall design. Pick one method and stick to it for all similar pieces.

Step 3: Measure and Mark Precisely — Use a measuring tape, a pencil, and ideally a dedicated drilling jig or template. A jig helps ensure that every hole is drilled in exactly the same spot, preventing visible inconsistencies. Measure from consistent edges or corners, and double-check your marks before drilling. Precision in measurement is a cornerstone of craftsmanship.

Step 4: Consider Scale and Proximity — Ensure the chosen hardware size is proportional to the cabinet door or drawer. A tiny knob on a large drawer can look awkward, just as an oversized pull on a small door can overpower it. Also, check that the hardware won't interfere with adjacent doors, countertops, or appliances when opened. There should be ample clearance.

Step 5: Create Pilot Holes and Drill Carefully — Always drill small pilot holes first. This prevents wood splitting and helps guide your main drill bit. Use a sharp drill bit that is slightly smaller than the screw diameter. Drill at a controlled speed, and use a piece of scrap wood as backing on the inside of the door or drawer to prevent splintering as the drill bit exits.

A kitchen island can be a game-changer for your Dallas–Fort Worth home, offering enhanced prep space, storage, and a gathering spot. But even with the best intentions, it's easy to make choices that can turn a dream feature into a functional headache. Drawing from over two decades of experience, AKN Design Group has seen common missteps that can be easily avoided with a little foresight and professional guidance.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Making the island too large. Many homeowners get excited about a big island, but an oversized island can overwhelm a kitchen, making circulation difficult. Relying on average clearances instead of measuring the narrowest points, especially with appliances open, can lead to cramped spaces. How to avoid it: Carefully measure your kitchen and mark out the island's footprint on the floor with painter's tape to visualize the space and ensure adequate circulation (at least 36 inches for basic passages, 42-48 inches for work aisles).

2. Blocking appliance doors or traffic routes. An island placed improperly can obstruct dishwasher, oven, or refrigerator doors, or become a roadblock in the main kitchen pathways. This disrupts workflow and creates frustrating bottlenecks. How to avoid it: Always consider the swing of appliance doors and the natural flow of traffic through the kitchen during the design phase. Use existing appliances to test clearances before finalizing the layout.

3. Failing to account for seating overhang. If your island includes seating, neglecting to plan for sufficient overhang means uncomfortable perching rather than relaxed dining. It's a detail often overlooked but crucial for comfort. How to avoid it: Plan for at least a 10-12 inch overhang for comfortable seating. Test with stools pulled out to ensure they don't block pathways or other kitchen functions.

4. Selecting an island depth that doesn't support its intended use. A standard island depth might be 24-36 inches, but if you're installing a sink, cooktop, or specialized storage, you'll likely need more. An inadequate depth compromises functionality. How to avoid it: Clearly define all intended uses for your island, then ensure the depth accommodates appliances, plumbing, and storage configurations effectively. Deeper shelves are only useful if the full depth remains visible.

5. Ignoring utility needs. Adding a sink, dishwasher, or cooktop to an island requires plumbing, electrical, and potentially gas lines.
